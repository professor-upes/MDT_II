What Is a Platform Business Model?

🏛️ The Core Definition

A platform is an intermediary that creates value by facilitating interactions between two or more distinct user groups — rather than producing and selling a product itself. The platform does not own the inventory or create the core value; it orchestrates the conditions for others to do so.

🏪 Think of it this way...

A traditional business is like a restaurant — it buys ingredients, cooks food, and sells meals. A platform is like a food court — it doesn't cook anything, but by bringing together restaurants and hungry customers in one space, it creates value for both sides.

🔄 Pipeline vs. Platform

🏭 Pipeline (Traditional)

  • Value created through a linear sequence: design → produce → sell
  • Firm owns inventory and assets
  • Scales by adding resources and capacity
  • Revenue from selling products
  • Examples: Tata Steel, Hindustan Unilever, Maruti Suzuki

🌐 Platform (Ecosystem)

  • Value created by enabling exchanges between producers & consumers
  • Firm orchestrates access to external assets
  • Scales by adding participants
  • Revenue from enabling transactions
  • Examples: Uber, Airbnb, Zomato, Amazon Marketplace

🔑 Key Takeaway: The firm's role shifts from maker to matchmaker. Platform value grows with participants, not production capacity. The most valuable companies in the world today — Google, Amazon, Meta — are all platforms.

Scaling the Old Way — Pipelines Grow by Adding Capacity

🍛 The dhaba that wants to double

A dhaba serves 200 plates a day. To serve 400, it needs more cooks, more burners, more tables, more space and more stock. Every extra plate has to be paid for in advance — before a single extra customer walks in.

Capacity: the most you can produce with the assets you own Fixed cost: what you pay even if you sell nothing (rent, plant, salaries) Variable cost: what each extra unit costs (raw material, fuel)

Scaling the Matchmaker Way — Asset-Light Growth

🍽️ The food court that wants to double

A food court that wants twice the diners doesn't hire chefs — it adds counters that other people run. Maruti adds a plant; Zomato adds a city. The app, the payment system and the rating system it already built work in the new city on day one.

Asset-light: growing without owning the main assets that do the work Marginal cost: the cost of serving one more customer

🏙️ Entering a New City — What Each Model Must Add

🍳 A restaurant chain

  • Lease outlets, fit out kitchens
  • Hire and train chefs and staff
  • Buy equipment and stock
  • Wait months to break even per outlet

📱 Zomato

  • Sign up restaurants that already exist
  • Onboard delivery partners paid per order
  • Run local marketing
  • Reuse the same app, payments and ratings everywhere

🔑 Key Takeaway: Platforms grow by recruiting other people's assets — their kitchens, cars, rooms and shops — and reusing one piece of software everywhere. That is why their cost curve flattens while a pipeline's keeps climbing.

A mall lets brands run the shops. But if parking is chaotic, shoppers blame the mall, not the brands. So the mall takes over parking, security and cleaning itself. When the customer's promise depends on something the platform doesn't control, the platform starts owning that piece.

Network Effects — The Engine of Platform Economics

📈 The Core Concept

A network effect exists when each additional user makes the platform more valuable for all other users. This is the single most important concept in platform economics — it creates a self-reinforcing growth loop.

📞 The Telephone Analogy

The first telephone was useless — there was nobody to call. The second telephone made the first one valuable. By the time millions had telephones, each new phone added value for everyone already on the network. This is a network effect in its purest form.

🔀 Types of Network Effects

👈👉 Same-Side (Direct)

  • More users on one side benefit others on the same side
  • WhatsApp: more friends = more useful
  • LinkedIn: more professionals = richer network
  • Stack Overflow: more developers = better answers

↔️ Cross-Side (Indirect)

  • More users on one side benefit users on the other side
  • More restaurants on Zomato → better for diners
  • More diners on Zomato → more attractive for restaurants
  • More apps on Play Store → more Android buyers

⚠️ Negative Effects

  • More users can reduce value
  • Surge pricing congestion on Ola/Uber
  • Content overload on Instagram
  • Spam & fake listings on OLX
  • Managed via curation, moderation, filters

🔑 Key Takeaway: Network effects are the moat. They create winner-take-most dynamics. But not all network effects are equally strong — the critical question is whether your users can easily multi-home (use competing platforms simultaneously).

Why Bigger Gets Better — Two Kinds of Scale

🛒 Big Bazaar vs. WhatsApp

A big supermarket gets cheaper per item as it grows — it buys in bulk. But your soap doesn't become more useful because a lakh other people bought it. WhatsApp is different: it gets more useful to you every time a friend joins. One kind of scale cuts cost; the other raises value.

Supply-side scale: cost per unit falls as you produce more Demand-side scale (network effect): value per user rises as more users join

🏭 Pipelines: Supply-Side Scale

  • Maruti: more cars → cheaper parts per car
  • HUL: bigger volumes → better rates from suppliers and media
  • Tata Steel: bigger furnaces → lower cost per tonne
  • Value to each customer stays the same

🌐 Platforms: Both Kinds at Once

  • Zomato: more restaurants → better choice for diners
  • Naukri: more candidates → more recruiters pay to search
  • Google Maps: more phones → better live traffic for everyone
  • Value rises while cost per user falls
PlatformCost per user falls because…Value per user rises because…
RedBusSame booking engine serves every routeMore operators → more routes and times to choose from
YouTubeServers and recommendation system are sharedMore creators → something for every niche
Amazon MarketplaceWarehouses and delivery routes get denserMore sellers → wider range and sharper prices
PractoOne app serves every cityMore doctors and reviews → easier, safer choice

🔑 Key Takeaway: Pipelines scale on one engine (cheaper to make). Platforms scale on two (cheaper to serve + more valuable to join). That double engine explains why platforms can outgrow pipelines — but the parts that still need people and vehicles, like delivery, scale like a pipeline.

The Principal-Agent Problem

🤝 The Classical Framework

The principal-agent problem arises when one party (the agent) acts on behalf of another (the principal), but their interests don't perfectly align — and the principal can't fully observe the agent's actions.

PROBLEM 1

🔒 Information Asymmetry

The agent knows more than the principal about the transaction, the market, or the quality of what is being offered. The principal is operating with incomplete information.

PROBLEM 2

⚖️ Misaligned Incentives

What is best for the agent (quick sale, higher commission) is not necessarily what is best for the principal (best price, best fit, best quality).

🚌 The Travel Agent Example — Before RedBus

🎫 Booking a bus in India, circa 2005

You walk into a local travel agent's office. You want a bus to Manali. The agent tells you "Sharma Travels has a seat, ₹800." But you don't know: Are there cheaper operators? Better buses? Is Sharma paying this agent the highest commission? The agent has information you don't, and their incentive is to sell what earns them the most — not what's best for you.

You are the principal. The agent is... the agent. And the system is working against you.

📊 Principal-Agent Relationships Across Industries

Domain Principal (You) Agent (Acting for You) The Conflict
Travel Passenger Local travel agent Agent steers to highest-commission operator
Real Estate Home buyer Property broker Broker wants quick sale, not best price for buyer
Financial Products Investor Insurance/MF agent Agent pushes highest-commission fund
Healthcare Patient Doctor (in fee-for-service) Incentive for unnecessary tests/procedures
Taxis Rider Taxi dispatcher Dispatcher sends closest driver, not best driver

🔑 Key Takeaway: The principal-agent problem is fundamentally about trust under opacity. Whenever you rely on someone who knows more than you and whose incentives differ from yours, you're exposed to this problem.

The Principal-Agent Problem in Everyday Life

🥦 Sending your younger brother to the sabzi mandi

You give him ₹500 for vegetables. He returns with average tomatoes, no change, and a new packet of chips. Did he compare shops? Bargain? You'll never know. He saw the market; you didn't. And his goals (finish fast, buy chips) are not your goals (fresh, cheap).

You are the principal — the person whose money and interest are at stake. He is the agent — the person acting for you.

🤔 Then Why Did We Use Agents at All?

🧠 They had knowledge

🔑 They had access

🔑 Key Takeaway: Agents were not villains — they were the only route to information. Their power, and their extra profit, came from the gap between what they knew and what you knew. Close that gap and their power shrinks.

Why Middlemen Existed — The Five Inefficiencies

🧭 Your first trip to Chandni Chowk's wholesale market

You don't know which lane sells what, what the fair price is, which shop is honest, which supplier fits your small order, or how to pay and ship. A local guide solves all five problems — and charges you for each one, whether you notice or not.

Inefficiency (friction): anything that makes a deal slower, costlier or riskier than it needs to be.
FRICTION 1

🔎 Search Cost

Time and effort to find your options.

  • Visiting ten shops for one fridge
  • Who profited: the shopkeeper you reached first
FRICTION 2

🔒 Information Gap

One side knows more about price or quality.

  • A farmer not knowing prices in other mandis
  • Who profited: the mandi commission agent
FRICTION 3

🤝 Trust Gap

You can't be sure the other side will deliver.

  • Paying an advance to an unknown contractor
  • Who profited: the "known person" who vouched
FRICTION 4

🧩 Matching Cost

Finding the right fit, not just any option.

  • Right job for a fresher; right match for marriage
  • Who profited: placement consultants, marriage bureaus
FRICTION 5

🧾 Transaction Cost

Effort to pay, sign, deliver and complete the deal.

  • Cheques, paperwork, physical forms, cash handling
  • Who profited: agents who "handled the formalities"
THE PATTERN

💡 Every fee had a reason

A broker's fee was really a price on search, information, trust and paperwork — bundled into one number you couldn't question.

🔑 Key Takeaway: Every middleman's fee was a price on one or more of these five frictions. Remove the friction — and the fee loses its reason to exist.

How Platforms Solved the Agency Problem

💡 The Platform Solution: Transparency

Platforms solved the principal-agent problem by disintermediating the agent and making information transparent. When you can see all operators, all prices, all ratings yourself — you no longer need to trust someone whose incentives don't match yours.

🔄 Before & After: The Disintermediation Pattern

Domain Old Agent (Opaque) Platform (Transparent)
🚌 Travel Local agent with hidden commissions RedBus / MakeMyTrip — all options visible
🚕 Taxis Dispatcher choosing which driver gets the fare Uber / Ola — algorithmic matching, transparent pricing
🏠 Real Estate Broker controlling property information 99acres / MagicBricks — direct listings
🛍️ Retail Salesperson steering to high-margin products Amazon / Flipkart — reviews, comparisons, full catalogue
💰 Finance Agent pushing highest-commission MF/insurance PolicyBazaar / Groww — comparison engines

⚠️ But Did the Problem Really Disappear?

🚨 Critical Insight: The principal-agent problem didn't disappear — it shape-shifted. The platform itself became the new agent. Its curation algorithms, ranking systems, and ad placements became the new information asymmetry. When "Top Result" means "paid placement" rather than "best for you," the platform's incentives have diverged from yours — the same structural problem, just at algorithmic scale.

🏪 Old Agency Problem

  • Travel agent steers you to Sharma Travels
  • Information hidden by a human
  • You can't see alternatives
  • Agent earns commission

💻 New Agency Problem

  • Platform shows "Sponsored" result first
  • Information shaped by an algorithm
  • You see alternatives but they're ranked by revenue
  • Platform earns ad/promotion fees

🔑 Key Takeaway: Platforms made you your own agent — but you're not a very good agent for yourself. You satisfice, you're influenced by defaults, and you're subject to decision fatigue. The agency problem evolved, not vanished.

Disintermediation Decoded — Remove, Replace, Re-intermediate

Intermediary: anyone standing between the producer and the customer Disintermediation: cutting that middle layer out Re-intermediation: a new, different middle layer taking its place
🚆 Train tickets in India

First, the tout outside the station sold "confirmed" tickets at a premium. Then IRCTC's website let you book directly — the tout was removed. Today many travellers book through apps like ixigo or ConfirmTkt, which sit between you and IRCTC and help with search and waitlist predictions — a new middleman, but a far cheaper and more transparent one.

➡️ Move 1: Go Direct

  • Airlines sell on their own websites and apps
  • D2C brands sell through their own online stores
  • Mutual funds offer "direct plans" with no distributor commission
  • Works when the producer has a strong brand customers search for

🔄 Move 2: Replace with a Platform

  • One digital middle layer serves lakhs of producers and crores of customers
  • Cost per transaction falls to a tiny fraction of a human agent's
  • Works when customers want choice and comparison
  • RedBus, MakeMyTrip, PolicyBazaar, Naukri

🔑 Key Takeaway: Most "disintermediation" stories are really re-intermediation: a costly, opaque human middleman is replaced by a cheap, transparent digital one that serves millions at once. The middle layer survives — its economics change completely.

Where the Value Went — Winners, Losers & the New Toll Booth

🛣️ The hidden toll booth

Before RedBus, the agent's commission was a toll you paid without seeing the toll booth. After RedBus, part of that toll went back into your pocket, part went to the platform, and the agent's booth closed. The value didn't vanish — it was redistributed.

🏆 Who Gained

  • Customers: lower prices, more choice, less effort
  • Small producers: a bus operator in Nashik or a home baker in Dehradun becomes visible far beyond their street
  • Platforms: a small cut on a huge number of transactions

📉 Who Lost

  • Local agents and brokers who lived on the information gap
  • Producers who hid behind opacity — poor quality is now exposed by ratings
  • Shops with no digital presence

🔀 What Moved

  • Power moved from whoever held information to whoever holds attention and data
  • The platform's ranking became the new toll booth — visible, but still a toll

🔑 Key Takeaway: Disintermediation redistributes value: customers save, small producers gain reach, and the single biggest share often ends with whoever controls discovery. That is why the platform itself became the "new agent" we saw earlier.

The Bottleneck Shift — From Scarcity to Overload

🔄 The Migrating Bottleneck

A critical insight in platform economics: solving one constraint simply reveals the next. The bottleneck in commerce never disappears; it migrates. Understanding where the bottleneck currently sits separates companies that build the right product from those solving yesterday's problem.

💡 Sandeep Paul's Insight

"The bottleneck has shifted to a new place." Platforms solved the information access problem but created the information overload and decision complexity problem. Each era's solution becomes the next era's constraint.

📊 Four Eras of the Bottleneck

Era The Bottleneck Who Solved It New Problem Created
🏪 Pre-Internet Access to goods Retail chains, logistics networks Limited information about alternatives
🌐 Web 1.0 / 2.0 Access to information Platforms (RedBus, Amazon, MakeMyTrip) Too many options → decision fatigue
📱 Current Processing capacity Filters, reviews, algorithms Platform incentives ≠ user interests
🤖 Emerging Taste, judgment & identity AI agents (in progress) Loss of serendipity, preference lock-in

🧠 The Current Bottleneck: Decision Overload

🔍 Example: Searching "Delhi to Manali bus" on RedBus

You get 150+ results. Now you must become the analyst: filter by time, price, operator rating, bus type, seat position, cancellation policy. Multiply this across every purchase decision in your life.

Result: You don't optimise — you satisfice. You pick "good enough" and move on. You're influenced by default sorting, sponsored listings, and decision fatigue.

🔑 Key Takeaway: The bottleneck migrated from "I can't find what I need" to "I have too many options and not enough cognitive bandwidth to evaluate them." This is the gap that creates demand for AI-mediated commerce.

Thinking in Bottlenecks — A Tool You Can Use

🌉 The narrowest bridge on the highway

A six-lane highway moves only as fast as its one-lane bridge. Widen that bridge and traffic flows — until the jam simply moves to the next narrow point. In business, money is made by whoever widens (or owns) the bridge that is narrowest right now.

Bottleneck: the single narrowest point that limits how much value the whole system can deliver — whether that's goods, information, trust or speed.

🧭 The Three-Step Method

1

Find the narrowest point

Ask: "What stops the customer from getting value faster, cheaper or more safely?"

Not the biggest problem — the one that limits everything else.

2

See who removes it

Which company or technology is widening that point?

That player usually earns the largest share of new value.

3

Follow where it moves next

Once removed, the constraint shifts somewhere else.

The next opportunity sits at the new narrow point.

💳 Worked Example — Payments in India

StageThe narrowest pointWho widened itWhere it moved next
1Getting cash at all — few bank branches and ATMsBanks expanding ATMs and accountsShops still needed cash; cards rarely accepted
2Acceptance — card machines were costly for small shopsUPI with a printed QR code that costs a shop almost nothingEveryone can pay; now the question is trust and credit
3 (today)Fraud prevention, and credit for small borrowers and shopsBanks, fintech lenders, payment apps — still in progressThe race is on

🔑 Key Takeaway: Don't start with "what's a good business?" Start with "where is the narrowest point right now, and who is widening it?" Strategy is the art of arriving at the next bottleneck before everyone else.

💧 Water in the desert

In a city with taps everywhere, nobody pays much for water. In a desert, whoever owns the only well sets the price. Value always collects at the point that is scarce — and when that point stops being scarce, the profits move with it.

🔑 Key Takeaway: When a bottleneck is solved, the profits that sat there migrate to the next scarce point. Managers who think in bottlenecks invest where value is about to move — not where it used to be.

Creating a Platform — Solving the Cold Start Problem

🐔🥚 The Chicken-and-Egg Challenge

Every platform faces the same existential question at birth: how do you attract one side when the other side isn't there yet? No drivers means no riders. No riders means no drivers. This is the cold start problem — and most platforms die here.

🛠️ Four Proven Launch Strategies

STRATEGY 1

💸 Subsidise One Side

Pay one side to show up so the other side has a reason to come. The subsidised side is your loss leader.

  • Uber India: Guaranteed minimum earnings for early drivers before rider demand existed
  • Paytm: Cashback for both merchants and users to bootstrap UPI adoption
  • PhonePe: Heavy cashback in the early UPI years
⚠️ Risk: Subsidy addiction — users leave when incentives stop
STRATEGY 2

🌱 Seed Value Yourself

Create the initial supply yourself so the demand side sees value from day one.

  • Reddit: Founders created early posts under fake accounts
  • Quora: Founders personally answered hundreds of questions
  • Zomato (early days): Team manually scanned and uploaded restaurant menus across Delhi
✅ Low cost, high credibility — but doesn't scale
STRATEGY 3

🧑‍💻 Single-Player Use Case

Build a tool that's useful to one side even without the other side. Then add the marketplace layer.

  • OpenTable: Gave restaurants reservation management software — valuable before any diners joined
  • Razorpay: Payment tools useful to merchants even before marketplace effects kicked in
  • Shopify: Store builder for merchants; marketplace came later
✅ Most sustainable — creates real value before network effects
STRATEGY 4

🔗 Piggyback an Existing Network

Attach to a platform that already has both sides and siphon users to yours.

  • PayPal: Integrated into eBay's buyer-seller network
  • Zynga: Its games grew on Facebook's social graph
  • Airbnb: Cross-posted listings to Craigslist to capture existing demand
⚠️ Risk: Host platform may cut you off (Zynga + Facebook)

🔑 Key Takeaway: The cold start is the #1 killer of platforms. The smartest founders don't try to build both sides simultaneously — they make one side inevitable, then let the other follow.

Cold Start, Deeper — Five More Ways to Fill an Empty Room

🍽️ The empty-restaurant problem

Walking past two restaurants — one packed, one empty — you pick the packed one, even if the empty one is better. A new platform is the empty restaurant. The four classic strategies help; these five tactics are what founders actually combine in practice.

TACTIC 5

📍 Start Tiny

Win one neighbourhood completely before expanding. A small area that feels "full" beats a big one that feels empty.

  • Swiggy began in Koramangala, Bengaluru
  • Zepto began in Mumbai
  • Facebook began at one university
TACTIC 6

⭐ Sign an Anchor Tenant

Like a new mall signing a multiplex first — one big draw pulls in everyone else.

  • JioCinema streamed IPL 2023 free to pull in viewers
  • Marketplaces sign big brands before festive sales
TACTIC 7

🛠️ Do It Manually First

Do by hand what the software will later do — it proves demand and sets quality.

  • Zomato's founders typed up restaurant menus themselves
  • Urban Company trained and verified its early professionals
TACTIC 8

🎯 Win the Harder Side First

Usually the supply side — drivers, restaurants, sellers. Demand follows good supply.

  • Swiggy brought its own delivery riders so restaurants without delivery staff could join
  • Ride-hailing apps recruited drivers before riders
TACTIC 9

🎟️ Make It Exclusive

Limit who can join so being inside feels valuable — and quality stays high early.

  • CRED launched only for people with high credit scores
  • Invite-only launches create waiting lists
ORIGIN STORY

🚌 Solve Your Own Problem

RedBus began after its founder couldn't get a bus ticket home from Bengaluru during Diwali — the principal-agent problem from Slide 9, felt first-hand.

🔑 Key Takeaway: No founder fills the whole restaurant on day one. They fill one table so well it looks busy — one neighbourhood, one anchor, one side — and let the crowd do the rest.

🔥 Lighting a chulha

You don't light big logs with a matchstick. You start with paper and twigs, blow on it, and only add logs once the fire holds on its own. Subsidies and manual effort are the paper and twigs; network effects are the logs.

Ecosystem Design — Roles, Architecture & Value Flow

🏗️ The Ecosystem Map

A platform ecosystem is not just the platform and its two sides. It's a layered network of roles, each extracting and contributing value differently. Understanding these roles is essential for both creators and participants.

ROLE 1

👑 Platform Orchestrator

Sets the rules, owns the infrastructure, governs interactions, captures the largest share of value.

  • Examples: Apple (App Store), Amazon (Marketplace), Google (Play Store)
  • Power: Controls access, pricing, visibility
ROLE 2

🧩 Complementors

Build products/services on top of the platform that make it more valuable — without owning the platform.

  • Examples: App developers on iOS, sellers on Amazon, restaurants on Zomato
  • Power: Depends on substitutability — are you one of thousands or one of a kind?
ROLE 3

🔧 Infrastructure Providers

Enable the platform to function — payments, logistics, cloud, identity verification.

  • Examples: Razorpay (payments), AWS (cloud), Delhivery (logistics), Aadhaar (identity)
  • Power: Often invisible but deeply embedded — high switching costs

💰 Value Flow in an Ecosystem

🌊 The River Analogy

Think of value as water flowing through a river system. The orchestrator builds the dam — they control the flow and take the biggest share. Complementors are tributaries — they add water (value) to the system. Infrastructure providers are the riverbed — invisible but without them, nothing flows. The question for any firm is: where in this river system do you want to sit, and can you control a chokepoint?

📊 Value Capture by Role — Who Wins?

RoleValue CreatedValue CapturedVulnerability
OrchestratorModerate (enables, doesn't produce)Highest (commission, data, attention)Regulatory action, platform alternatives
ComplementorHigh (makes platform useful)Low–Medium (depends on differentiation)Platform competing with you, commoditisation
Infrastructure ProviderCritical (enables everything)Medium (B2B pricing power)Standardisation, open-source alternatives

🔑 Key Takeaway: The orchestrator captures disproportionate value relative to what they create. The strategic question is not just "can we build a platform?" but "can we control a chokepoint in the value flow?"

Platform Monetisation — Who Pays and Why

💰 The Fundamental Pricing Question

Platforms serve multiple sides. The question isn't "what do we charge?" — it's "which side do we charge, which side do we subsidise, and why?" Get this wrong and you kill the network effect before it starts.

🎪 The Nightclub Analogy

Nightclubs often let women in free and charge men a cover fee. Why? Because the presence of women attracts the paying side. The subsidised side is the one whose presence makes the platform valuable to the side that pays. Platforms follow the same logic — subsidise the side you need more of, charge the side that extracts more value.

Monetisation, Simplified — Five Levers as Everyday Things

🏪 One street, five ways to earn

Imagine you own the busiest street in town. You could charge a toll to pass, rent billboards, run a members-only club, sell a footfall report to brands, or rent out power sockets to stalls. Platforms earn in exactly these five ways.

LEVER 1 · TOLL BOOTH

💳 Transaction Fee

A small cut every time a deal passes through. Also called the take rate — the % the platform keeps.

  • Zomato's commission from restaurants
  • Razorpay's fee per payment
  • Urban Company's commission per job
LEVER 2 · BILLBOARD

📢 Advertising

Pay to be seen first on a busy road. The more people pass by, the more the billboard is worth.

  • Google search ads
  • Amazon "Sponsored" products
  • Zomato promoted restaurants
LEVER 3 · CLUB MEMBERSHIP

⭐ Premium Access

A recurring fee for better access, perks or tools — like a gym or club card.

  • LinkedIn Premium
  • Zomato Gold
  • Naukri plans for recruiters
LEVER 4 · MARKET REPORT

📊 Data & Insights

Selling what the platform learns — trends, demand, audience insight — usually in aggregate, never personal details.

  • Bloomberg terminals
  • Restaurant and seller dashboards showing demand trends
LEVER 5 · POWER SOCKET

🧩 API Access

Charging others to plug their products into your system. API = a socket that lets one software connect to another.

  • Razorpay and Stripe payment APIs
  • Twilio messaging
  • Zerodha's Kite Connect trading API
THE REAL WORLD

🧱 Platforms Stack Levers

Zomato uses a toll (commission), a billboard (promoted listings), a club (Gold) — plus a small platform fee per order, about ₹14.9 in major cities in mid-2026.

🔑 Key Takeaway: Every platform revenue line is one of five familiar things: a toll, a billboard, a club, a report or a socket. Mature platforms stack several — so no single lever has to carry the whole business.

Who Should Pay? — A Three-Question Decision Helper

🎪 The college fest

Students get in free. Sponsors pay for banners, food stalls pay for counters. Why not charge students? Because students are the reason sponsors come. Charge them, and the sponsors have nobody to reach.

1

Who needs whom more?

The side that's harder to attract usually enters free or cheap.

2

Who gains most per deal?

The side that earns money from each transaction can afford to pay.

3

Who leaves if charged?

Price-sensitive sides walk away fast — don't charge them first.

PlatformFree or cheap sidePaying sideHow the three questions explain it
💼 NaukriJob seekersRecruitersCandidates are what recruiters need; a hire is worth far more to a company than a fee
🎬 YouTubeViewersAdvertisers (creators share ad revenue)Viewers would leave if charged; advertisers gain from their attention
🔧 Urban CompanyCustomers pay only the service priceProfessionals pay a commissionProfessionals gain steady jobs they couldn't find alone
🛵 RapidoRidersDrivers, via a flat subscription instead of a per-ride cutDrivers use several apps — a predictable fee helps keep them
📈 ZerodhaLong-term investors (no brokerage on delivery)Active traders (flat fee per order)Traders gain most per trade; investors bring trust and assets
⚠️ The classic mistake: charging the side you need most, too early. The flywheel never starts. The opposite mistake — charging nobody for too long — builds a crowd with no business underneath it.

🔑 Key Takeaway: Pricing a platform is not about the product — it's about balance. Keep the side you need most coming in; charge the side that gains most from meeting them.

Zerodha Through the Digital Transformation Lens

🏦 From the branch with a relationship manager to an app on your phone

Traditional brokers ran branches, relationship managers and paper forms, and charged a percentage of every trade to pay for them. Zerodha asked: if software can do the branch's job, why should the customer pay for the branch? The answer became its price — and its price became its marketing.

🔓 Digital Transformation Unlock

📉 Cost side — what technology removed or shrank

  • No branch network or relationship managers
  • Paperless onboarding through e-KYC
  • In-house software instead of costly licensed systems
  • Free education reduces support load — informed users ask fewer basic questions
  • Word of mouth replaces most advertising spend

📈 Revenue side — what technology made possible

  • Flat fees at huge volume — tiny per order, large in total
  • Interest income from margin trading and client funds

A revenue stream tied to one activity is fragile.
