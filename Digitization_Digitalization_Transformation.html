Digitization, Digitalization & Digital Transformation

Three words that are not synonyms

🎯 Core Insight

  • We digitize information, we digitalize processes and roles, and we digitally transform the business and its strategy.
  • Each term names a different scope of change — and scope decides the budget, the sponsor, the timeline and the measure of success.
  • Using them interchangeably is not a vocabulary problem. It is a governance problem.

Why the Distinction Is Not Pedantry

⚠️ Two Expensive Errors

Calling small change "transformation"

  • A scanning or ERP project is sold to the board as transformation
  • Expectations are set at the level of competitive repositioning
  • What is delivered is a searchable archive
  • The project succeeds on its own terms and is judged a failure
  • Conclusion drawn: "digital doesn't work here"

Handing transformation to IT

  • The word sounds technical, so ownership lands with the CIO
  • IT can deliver systems — it cannot rewrite a business model
  • It has no mandate over pricing, incentives or structure
  • The programme stalls at the edge of IT's authority
  • Nobody is accountable for the change that mattered

📌 What Scope Actually Decides

  • Who sponsors it — a function head, the COO, or the CEO and board
  • What it costs and how long it runs
  • What governance it needs — project, programme, or continuous strategic agenda
  • What counts as success — retrieval time, cycle time, or revenue mix

The budget gets approved on one premise and spent on another. Two years later the position is unchanged and the wrong lesson is learned.

The Standard Definitions

📖 Digitization — Gartner

"Digitization is the process of changing from analog to digital form, also known as digital enablement."

Gartner adds the decisive qualifier: it changes an analog process to digital form without any different-in-kind changes to the process itself.

📖 Digitalization — Gartner

"Digitalization is the use of digital technologies to change a business model and provide new revenue and value-producing opportunities; it is the process of moving to a digital business."

Jason Bloomberg flags this wording as part of the problem — it reads almost exactly like a definition of digital transformation. He argues the useful boundary is narrower: digitalization is about operations.

📖 The Formulation to Remember

"We digitize information, we digitalize processes and roles that make up the operations of a business, and we digitally transform the business and its strategy."

Jason Bloomberg

Three verbs, three objects. The object of the verb tells you which level you are on — information, operations, or the business itself.

Three Ascending Levels of Change

Digitization

Changes the format of information.
Analog → digital.

Digitalization

Changes operations and roles.
How work gets done.

Digital Transformation

Changes the business model and strategy.
What we sell, and to whom.

🔗 How the Levels Relate

  • Each level is necessary but not sufficient for the one above it.
  • You cannot digitalize a process whose information is still on paper.
  • You cannot transform a business whose operations are still manual.
  • The error firms make is not skipping a level — it is stopping at one and calling it the next.

Digitization and digitalization are essentially about technology. Digital transformation is not — it is about the customer.

Level 1 — Digitization

Encoding analog information into digital form

What changes

The format of information. Storage cost collapses, retrieval becomes instant, transmission becomes free, copies become perfect and unlimited.

What does not change

The process, the roles, the org chart, the business model. A scanned approval form is still routed, signed and filed by the same people in the same order.

Worked examples

  • Vinyl LP or cassette → audio file; VHS tape → video file
  • Paper contracts and case files → scanned PDFs, made searchable by OCR
  • Paper patient charts → electronic records; X-ray film → DICOM images
  • Handwritten machine logs → sensor telemetry from an instrumented line
  • Hand-drawn engineering drawings → CAD files

Why it still matters

  • It is the least glamorous level and the most frequently oversold.
  • But it creates the raw material — data — that Levels 2 and 3 depend on entirely.
  • A firm with its information still on paper has no path upward.

Level 2 — Digitalization

Putting digitized information to work

What changes

How work gets done. Workflows, decision rights, cycle times, cost-to-serve — and the definition of the jobs people hold.

What does not change

What the business sells and how it earns. The value proposition, the revenue model and the competitive position are untouched.

Worked examples

  • Self-service portals and chatbots absorbing the queue ahead of the call centre
  • Straight-through loan origination with automated credit decisioning and e-KYC
  • Demand forecasting and algorithmic replenishment replacing a planner's spreadsheet
  • Predictive maintenance triggered by sensor data, not a fixed service calendar
  • Live dashboards replacing the monthly management pack

The part most often missed: roles

  • Digitalization does not merely speed work up — it changes what people are for.
  • A claims clerk who keyed every claim becomes an exception handler.
  • A branch teller becomes an advisor.
  • That role shift is where most of the value and nearly all of the resistance live.

The characteristic failure: "paving the cow path" — automating a process instead of redesigning it. The result is a broken process that now fails faster, at scale, with a licence fee attached.

Level 3 — Digital Transformation

Changing what the business is, for the customer

What changes

The business and its strategy. What is sold, who it is sold to, how revenue is earned, how the organisation is structured and what it rewards.

What it is not

A technology programme, a project with an end date, an IT budget line, or a digitalization roadmap with a more ambitious cover page.

The questions it actually asks

  • What are we really selling? To whom? Why would they choose us?
  • How do we make money — and does that still work?
  • No technology decision answers any of these.

Two properties that set it apart

  • It is continuous. There is no go-live date after which the firm is transformed — which makes it a poor fit for conventional project governance.
  • It is owned by the business. A transformation sponsored by the CIO alone has already failed. It needs a mandate over incentives, structure and culture that no technology function holds.

Recurring patterns

  • Product → service: selling an outcome rather than an asset
  • Linear → platform: becoming the marketplace rather than the seller
  • Ownership → access: subscription and usage replacing one-time purchase
  • Product firm → data firm: monetising analytics, ad inventory, risk services
  • Channel → ecosystem: embedding your capability in someone else's journey via APIs

Test: Has what we sell, who we serve, or how we earn changed — and would a competitor have to change their business model to answer us?

A Three-Question Test for Any Initiative

Apply in order — the first "yes" tells you the level you are actually at

1. Did only the format of the information change?

The same people do the same steps in the same order — on a screen rather than on paper.

→ Digitization

2. Did the way work gets done change, while the business model stayed the same?

Steps removed, decisions automated, roles redefined — but the firm sells the same things to the same customers.

→ Digitalization

3. Did what we sell, who we serve, or how we earn change?

The value proposition or revenue model is different, and rivals cannot match it without changing their own.

→ Transformation

If nothing returns a "yes"

  • Then the initiative is a technology purchase.
  • That may still be a sound decision — but it should be justified and measured as one.
  • It should not be presented to a board as strategic change.

Use this on any case in the course, and on any programme you meet in practice — whatever the deck calls it.

Industry Scenarios

The same three rungs, traced through different sectors

Banking & Payments

Digitization

Loan files, signature cards and cheques imaged; statements moved from print to electronic records.

Digitalization

e-KYC, straight-through loan origination, automated fraud scoring, a mobile app. Still a bank selling deposits and credit.

Transformation

Open-banking APIs and banking-as-a-service; credit embedded in a retailer's checkout. Rivals become fintechs, not banks.

Retail & Grocery

Digitization

Barcoded SKU catalogues, digitized stock ledgers, product photography and price lists as data.

Digitalization

POS integrated to the warehouse, demand forecasting, digital loyalty, e-commerce bolted onto the store business.

Transformation

Omnichannel fulfilment and dark stores, plus a retail-media network selling ads against shopper data — a media business with a grocery attached.

Automotive & Manufacturing

Digitization

Paper drawings to CAD; handwritten shift and machine logs to sensor telemetry.

Digitalization

MES and SCADA on the line, digital twins, predictive maintenance, IIoT quality control. Cost per unit falls; the firm still sells machines.

Transformation

Selling uptime or output instead of the asset; software-defined products improved after sale by over-the-air updates.

Notice: Levels 1 and 2 look broadly similar across industries. Level 3 is where sectors diverge — because transformation is specific to a customer and a market, not to a technology.

Industry Scenarios (continued)

Media & Entertainment

Digitization

Film and tape masters encoded as files; print archives converted to digital text.

Digitalization

Online catalogue and queue management, automated logistics and recommendation on top of an existing distribution business.

Transformation

Distribution gives way to a subscription streaming relationship, then to original commissioning — owning demand rather than renting content.

Energy & Power

Digitization

Manual meter readings replaced by smart-meter data; asset and network records converted to digital form.

Digitalization

Automated billing, outage detection and grid analytics; field crews dispatched by algorithm rather than roster.

Transformation

From selling kilowatt-hours to selling energy outcomes — demand response, storage, EV charging, virtual power plants. The customer becomes a supplier too.

Healthcare

Digitization

Paper charts scanned into electronic records; X-ray film replaced by DICOM imaging.

Digitalization

Computerised order entry, e-prescribing, automated scheduling and claims, clinical decision support at the point of care.

Transformation

Telehealth-first pathways, remote monitoring, hospital-at-home — and reimbursement shifting from activity to outcomes.

Trace any case in this course through the three rungs before judging it. Most "digital failures" are Level 2 programmes that were sold as Level 3.

Where Organisations Go Wrong

Each is a mislabelling problem before it is an execution problem

Level 1 sold as Level 3

  • A scanning or migration project presented to the board as transformation. It delivers exactly what it promised and is still judged a failure.

Transformation delegated to IT

  • IT can deliver systems but holds no authority over pricing, channel strategy, incentives or organisational design — the levers transformation actually moves.

Technology bought before the process is redesigned

  • Automating an existing workflow preserves its defects and industrialises them. The tell: a business case built entirely on headcount reduction.

Adoption measured instead of outcomes

  • Logins, uploads and licences deployed are Level 1 measures. Reported against a transformation they describe activity, not change.

Culture treated as a communications workstream

  • Levels 2 and 3 both change what people's jobs are. Leave incentives and decision rights untouched and the old process runs quietly beside the new system.

Stopping at Level 2 and declaring victory

  • The most consequential error, because it is invisible from inside. The firm becomes efficient and digital-looking — at a business model a competitor is about to make irrelevant.

Excellent digitalization is exactly what a firm displaced by a new entrant usually has.

Discussion

💬 Questions for the Session

  • Take an initiative you have seen called a "digital transformation". Apply the three-question test. What level was it actually at, and what did the mislabelling cost?
  • Gartner's definition of digitalization mentions changing the business model — arguably that is transformation. Is the narrower reading right, or does the boundary dissolve under scrutiny?
  • Netflix and GE both invested heavily in digital. Using the three levels, account for why one is taught as a success and the other as a failure. Where did ambition and mandate part company?
  • Is digital transformation always "about the customer"? Name an industry where it was driven by regulation, cost or supply-side economics instead.
  • Can a firm reach Level 3 without completing Levels 1 and 2 — and is a digital-native entrant, which never had analog information to digitize, playing a different game altogether?
