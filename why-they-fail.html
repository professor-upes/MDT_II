Disruption Through Christensen's Lens

Large firms rarely lose because they are lazy or stupid. They lose because they keep doing, very well, what made them successful.

1 · The puzzle

Incumbents have more money, talent and customers, yet the challenger often wins

Kodak, 1975

A Kodak engineer, Steven Sasson, built one of the first digital cameras. It took 23 seconds to record a black-and-white image. Management's reaction was roughly: nice, but don't tell anyone. Film was where the profit was.

Kodak, 2012

Kodak filed for bankruptcy. It had the invention, the patents, the brand and the distribution. What it lacked was a reason, inside its own profit logic, to kill film before someone else did.

Think: if you had been Kodak's CFO in 1985, film earning fat margins and digital earning nothing, would you have funded digital? Be honest. That discomfort is the whole lecture.
Christensen's core claim (The Innovator's Dilemma, 1997): the practices that make firms great (listen to your best customers, invest where margins are highest, grow big markets) are exactly the practices that make them miss disruption. Good management is the cause, not the cure.

2 · The framework

Disruption happens because product improvement outruns what customers can use

① Overshoot

Incumbents improve faster than most customers need. Customers stop paying for extra performance.

② Foothold

An entrant serves customers the incumbent ignores, with a product that is worse on old measures but cheaper, simpler or more convenient.

③ Move upmarket

The entrant improves until it is "good enough" for mainstream customers. By then, the incumbent's cost structure can't follow it down.

Analogy: the incumbent is building a faster and faster train for the few passengers who want a 400 km/h ride. The entrant starts with an auto-rickshaw for people who had no ride at all. Ten years later, the rickshaw is an electric car and the train is half empty.

3 · What disruption is not

Not every successful newcomer is a disrupter, and Christensen himself said so

Christensen's own test (HBR, 2015)

In "What Is Disruptive Innovation?", Christensen, Raynor & McDonald argued that Uber is not disruptive: it did not start with non-consumers or the low end, and it offered a better service to mainstream taxi users. The same logic applies to Tesla, which entered at the high end, a sustaining move.

Disruption is a process: a foothold, then a move upmarket. It is not a label for anything new that hurts incumbents.

Why this matters in an exam

  • Jio (2016): free data, better network, mainstream customers from day one, funded by massive capital. Closr to a capital-funded sustaining attack than classic disruption.
  • Quick commerce vs kirana: faster and more convenient for urban mainstream buyers, subsidised by capital. Test both readings .
  • Christensen got the iPhone "wrong" in 2007: he saw it as a sustaining phone; its disruption was to the laptop, as a new-market device. Even the author had to apply the test carefully.
The four test questions: (1) Where did it start: low end, non-consumers, or the mainstream? (2) Was it worse on the incumbent's key measure at first? (3) Did the incumbent rationally ignore it? (4) Is the entrant's cost structure one the incumbent can't copy without hurting itself? Mostly yes means disruption. Mostly no means a strong competitor, perhaps capital-funded.

5 · Six reasons incumbents lose

Six rational behaviours that add up to an irrational outcome

How to use this: each reason maps to one part of RPV. Resource problems can be fixed with money. Process and value problems need structural answers, such as separate units, new metrics and new owners. That is why "just invest more" rarely works.

5 · Reasons 1 & 2

Managers defend today's revenue because that is what they are paid for, and sunk assets make the defence feel wise

1 · Existing P&L incentives

Mechanism: business-unit heads are judged on this year's revenue and margin. A new offer that cannibalises (eats into) their sales makes their numbers worse, so it gets starved of budget, attention or shelf space.

Global: Intel's CEO Paul Otellini later said he passed on making the iPhone's chip because the price and volume didn't fit Intel's margin model. Mobile went to ARM-based chips.

India: IT services firms bill largely by effort (people × hours). Generative AI that halves the effort also threatens to halve the bill, so the incentive is to adopt AI slowly for clients. Outcome-based pricing is the escape, but it hits this year's revenue.

Diagnostic: Who in your firm is personally worse off if the new model succeeds?

2 · Sunk-cost commitments

Mechanism: factories, branches, store leases and legacy IT represent huge past investment. Disruption often means writing them off, and no one wants to sign that write-off.

Global: Blockbuster's thousands of leased stores, with late fees as a major profit source. Its model depended on the thing customers hated most.

India: multiplex chains defended theatrical release windows as OTT grew; bank branch networks built for cash and paper; ICE-engine (petrol/diesel) plants and supplier networks for two-wheeler makers entering EVs.

Diagnostic: Which asset on our balance sheet would a new entrant be glad not to own?

The trap: "We've invested too much to change" is a statement about the past used to make a decision about the future. Economists call this the sunk-cost fallacy.
09
5 · Reasons 3 & 4

Listening perfectly to your best customers, and letting the middle protect its turf, blinds the firm to what comes next

3 · Customer captivity

Mechanism: Christensen's value network: a firm's priorities are set by the customers who pay its bills. Those customers want the current product, only better. Research tells you to ignore the cheap, "inferior" alternative.

Global: in Christensen's disk-drive study, leading makers of large drives listened to mainframe and minicomputer customers who didn't want smaller drives. The smaller drives won the next generation of computers, repeatedly.

India: full-service brokers served high-value clients who wanted research and relationship managers. First-time young investors wanted a cheap app. Zerodha's flat fee won that segment and became one of India's largest brokers.

Diagnostic: Which customers are we happy to lose, and who is happily taking them?

4 · Cultural antibodies

Mechanism: new ways of working threaten the power, skills and status of the middle layer. They rarely say no. They delay, add process and redirect resources.

Global: a study of Nokia (Vuori & Huy, Administrative Science Quarterly, 2016) found that fear across management layers led middle managers to tell leaders what they wanted to hear, and leaders to over-promise. The smartphone response stalled.

India: public-sector banks with large technology budgets but slow customer-facing change, and legacy media newsrooms resisting digital-first workflows.

Diagnostic: Whose job description disappears if this works, and have we given them a new one?

5 · Reasons 5 & 6

Approval chains built for factories can't keep pace with software, and neither can pay scales

5 · Slow decision cycles

Mechanism: capital-budgeting processes (business cases, committee approvals, annual budgets) were designed for irreversible, costly decisions such as a new plant. Digital experiments are cheap and reversible, but they go through the same machinery.

Global: Levi's once spent months planning and refining each initiative before launch; its digital shift required learning to ship early and iterate.

India: a startup can test a new lending product in weeks; a large lender may need months of credit-committee, IT and compliance sign-offs for the same pilot.

Diagnostic: How many signatures does a ₹10 lakh reversible experiment need here?

6 · Talent mismatch

Mechanism: digital talent expects equity-like upside, autonomy, fast shipping and tolerance for failure. Grade-based pay, hierarchy and punishment for failure repel them, or turn them into frustrated employees who leave.

Global: legacy automakers struggled to hire and keep software engineers for connected and electric cars, and several set up separate software units with different pay.

India: conglomerates building consumer apps (super-apps) have often had to create separate digital subsidiaries with startup-style pay and titles to attract product talent.

Diagnostic: Could our best digital hire earn more by failing at a startup than succeeding here?

7 · Newer cases

The pattern is repeating now, and some incumbents are fighting back successfully

Google & generative AI

Google researchers published the transformer architecture in 2017. OpenAI's ChatGPT (2022) turned it into the product that shook search. The Kodak pattern: an invention sat inside a firm whose search-advertising revenue a chat answer could cannibalise. Google has since responded with Gemini. The outcome is still open.

Indian IT services & AI

An effort-based billing model means AI productivity shrinks revenue per project (reason 1). Firms that reprice around outcomes and platforms may thrive. Those that protect headcount billing risk a Kodak moment.

Zerodha vs full-service brokers

A textbook low-end foothold: a flat fee, a self-service app, and first-time investors. Banks' broking arms responded late with discount plans that cannibalised their own fees.

EV two-wheelers: the incumbent comeback

Startups such as Ola Electric and Ather opened the market. By 2025, incumbents TVS (iQube) and Bajaj (Chetak) had overtaken Ola in monthly sales on service reach, reliability and dealer networks. Proof that incumbents can win when they commit early.

Adobe: self-disruption

Adobe moved from boxed Creative Suite to Creative Cloud subscriptions in 2012–13 and accepted a revenue dip, deliberately cannibalising its own licence sales before a cloud rival could.

Microsoft under Nadella

From 2014, Microsoft reframed itself from Windows licences to cloud (Azure) and Office subscriptions. Incentives and culture were rebuilt, not just products: a rare full RPV reset.

8 · Counter-strategies

Where to house a new venture depends on whether it fits your processes and your values

Adapted from Christensen & Overdorf (2000).

Rule of thumb: a disruptive business housed inside the mainstream will be judged by mainstream values and starved. The question is not "Should we do digital?" but "Where does it live, and whose metrics judge it?"

9 · Failure in digital transformation

Most digital transformations fail because firms use new technology to protect the old business model

The evidence

BCG's 2020 study "Flipping the Odds of Digital Transformation Success" found that about 70% of digital transformations fall short of their objectives. McKinsey's surveys report similar failure rates.

The Christensen diagnosis

Most "transformations" are really sustaining uses of digital technology: a better app, a faster process, the same business model. They improve the firm on today's measures, exactly the move Christensen predicts incumbents will make, and they leave the firm open to a model-level attack.
