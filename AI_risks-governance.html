Governance, AI Risk & Cybersecurity

Who may act — on what — with what checks?

Governance decides what is allowed. AI risk is what goes wrong when the technology behaves unexpectedly. Cybersecurity is what goes wrong when someone wants it to. All three answer one question: who can act, on what, with what checks.

The accidental failure

An LLM invents a refund policy and a customer relies on it. Nobody attacked anything. The system simply did something plausible and wrong.

The deliberate failure

An attacker hides instructions in a document your AI assistant reads, and the assistant follows them. Same system, same output channel — different intent behind it.

Every capability you add as a manager adds a way in. Attack surface simply means the total number of points where an outsider — or an accident — could reach your business.

The one-line version: governance = "who decides, using what rules, with what checks." If you cannot name the person, the rule and the check, you do not have governance — you have an intention.

Slide 6
Part III · AI Risk

The Six Risks — Grouped So They Stick

Structural — built into how GenAI works

1 · Deployment explosion

Used in every function at once. Nobody can pre-test every context of use. Mitigations designed for known cases miss the ones nobody imagined.

2 · Monitoring at scale

Unexpected behaviour compounds quietly. Without monitoring in the wild, you learn about failure from a customer, not a dashboard.

Behavioural — created by how humans use it

3 · Hallucinations

Confident, plausible, wrong. Untrained users do not verify. False data reaches client documents.

4 · Irresponsible prompting

Confidential data pasted into third-party tools; prompt injection steering the model from inside a document.

Organisational — created by how firms deploy it

5 · Multi-point ownership

Provider → enterprise → department → user. Each layer adds risk; no layer thinks it owns the whole.

6 · Training deficit

Narrow AI needed trained data scientists. GenAI needs every employee trained — a far larger, rarely funded task.

Teach the three buckets, not the six numbers. Structural risks you mitigate, behavioural risks you train, organisational risks you assign. Students remember three verbs far longer than six nouns.

Security Is the Enforcement Layer of Governance

A policy that says "confidential data must not leave the company" is governance. The control that makes it impossible is security. Without the second, the first is a wish.

Governance saysSecurity makes it real byWithout it
Only authorised staff see customer dataAccess control, least privilege, loggingEveryone can see everything; nobody knows who looked
Don't paste confidential data into AI toolsData-loss prevention, approved-tool allowlistsPolicy depends entirely on memory and goodwill
We must know when something goes wrongMonitoring, detection, incident alertingYou find out from a customer or a newspaper
Vendors must protect our dataContract clauses, audit rights, access reviewsTheir weakest engineer is now your risk
This slide is the hinge of the whole lecture. Everything before it was about deciding rules; everything after it is about making rules bite. Say that out loud.

What It Costs in India

IBM Cost of a Data Breach Report, India cut

Top way in

Phishing, including voice and SMS — 19% of Indian breaches. Someone clicked.

Costliest sector

Financial services at ₹40.9 cr, then technology and communications.

Biggest saving

Offensive testing — red teaming and penetration testing — saved ₹2.47 cr per breach.

Read the phishing number again. India's most expensive category of corporate failure begins, one time in five, with an employee clicking something. That is not a problem you can purchase your way out of.

Slide 12
Part IV · Cybersecurity

An Attack Is a Campaign, Not a Moment

Someone will always click. The business question is not whether stage 2 happens — it is how many weeks an attacker gets in stages 3 and 4 before anyone notices. India's average detect-and-contain window was 263 days. That is nine months of quiet access.

Part IV · Cybersecurity

Ransomware, and the Case That Made It Real in India

It is an industry, not a hoodie

  • Ransomware-as-a-Service: developers rent the malware to affiliates for a cut
  • Access brokers sell the way in; negotiators handle your call
  • They research your revenue and your insurance before naming a price

Why backups stopped being enough

  • Step 1 — encrypt: backups defeat this
  • Step 2 — steal first: backups do nothing; pay or we publish
  • Step 3 — pressure your customers and regulator directly

Case — AIIMS Delhi, November 2022

What happenedWhat it teaches
Ransomware hit India's flagship public medical institute. Admissions, discharge, billing and lab reports went down. The hospital ran on paper for weeks. Availability was the crisis, not confidentiality. A non-commercial institution is still a prime target. Recovery time, not ransom size, was the real cost.
Exercise that lands every time: AIIMS had no revenue to lose. Ask the class to cost the incident anyway. Ten minutes in, they discover cyber risk quantification is a judgement exercise, not an arithmetic one — which is exactly the point.

When AI Becomes the Attacker's Instrument

ExposureWhat it isWhy old controls fail
AI-enabled impersonation and deepfakesSynthetic video, voice or writing convincingly imitating a specific real personVerification by recognising a face, voice or writing style no longer proves identity
Prompt injectionHidden instructions placed inside a document, email or webpage that an AI system reads and obeysNo malware, no alarm — the system did exactly what it was designed to do: read and follow

Compromised data or modelsCorruption of what the system learns from or retrievesAn integrity failure: the system works, confidently, and is wrong

Governance without a control is a stated intention. A control without an accountable owner is operational theatre.

Remember — 26% of malicious breaches in India in 2026 were AI-generated (IBM, August 2026). This is no longer an emerging category. It is a quarter of what is actually happening.

India's Compliance Stack — and the Six-Hour Clock

InstrumentBindsCore obligation
DPDP Act 2023Anyone processing personal digital dataConsent, purpose limits, safeguards, breach notification, significant penalties

Six hours is among the tightest reporting windows in the world. Meeting it requires a pre-written runbook naming who decides, who drafts and who signs. Compliance here is an operational readiness question, not a legal one.

Part V · Putting It Together

Who Owns All Of This

First line — the business

Owns the risk it creates. The head of sales who approves a new CRM owns that CRM's risk. Not IT.

Second line — risk & security

Sets policy, advises, challenges, monitors. Advises — does not own.

Third line — internal audit

Independently assures the board that the first two lines actually work in practice.

What the board answers for

  • Setting risk appetite — how much risk is acceptable for the return
  • Ensuring resourcing matches the stated appetite (this is the usual gap)
  • Reviewing incidents and the lessons actually taken from them
  • Confirming a response plan exists and has been tested, not merely written
The sentence that fails a board, in any jurisdiction: "we delegated it to the CISO." Delegating execution is proper. Delegating accountability is not recognised by regulators, courts, or insurers.

Part V · Close

Decision Principles to Leave With

  • Capability and exposure move together. Every transformation win adds a door. Choose knowingly.
  • Availability is the business risk. What stops Indian firms is systems going dark, not only data leaking.
  • Detection speed decides cost. Everyone is breached eventually; the 263-day firm and the 3-day firm end up somewhere very different.
  • Your controls end where your vendors begin — your liability does not. Security is decided in procurement.
  • AI made impersonation cheap. Any control resting on recognising a face or a voice has already expired.
  • Risk moved to the user. When outputs depend on prompts, training stops being HR and becomes a control.
  • Six hours. India's reporting clock makes a rehearsed plan a compliance requirement, not a nicety.
  • Governance is the differentiator. NIST added a Govern function because the failures were managerial. That is your job, not the CISO's.

You will not be hired to configure a firewall or fine-tune a model. You will be hired to decide how much risk the business accepts, to fund the controls that match that decision, and to be in the room — able to ask the right question — when something goes wrong.

Exit question: if you could implement exactly one control across your organisation tomorrow — one, not three — what would it be, and what are you consciously choosing to leave unprotected?
Do not resolve the exit question. Let them leave with it open. The students who come back next week still arguing about it are the ones who understood the lecture.
