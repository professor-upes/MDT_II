02
Hook

A truck that reports its own health changes who the customer calls, and what they pay for

2010: the unconnected truck

The fleet owner buys a truck. It breaks down on the highway. The driver calls the owner, the owner calls a mechanic, and the truck sits idle for three days. The manufacturer hears nothing until a service visit, if there is one.

Today: the connected truck

Sensors report engine temperature, fuel use and location. The manufacturer's system spots a failing part two weeks early and books a service slot. The owner pays a subscription for uptime, and the manufacturer now knows how every truck is really used.

Think before we start: in the second story, what exactly is the manufacturer selling: a truck, data, or kilometres without breakdowns? Write down one word. We will return to it on slide 09.
03
1 · Why now

IoT and Big Data became affordable because sensors, bandwidth and storage all got cheap at once

Compute (Moore's Law)

Chips small and cheap enough to put in a meter, a tractor or a milk can.

Bandwidth (Butter's Law)

Mobile data became cheap. In India, the 2016 data price collapse put connectivity in every district.

Storage (Kryder's Law)

Keeping years of sensor readings now costs almost nothing, so firms keep everything and analyse later.

Link to our Laws lecture: no single technology caused the IoT wave. What caused it is that the three costs fell together. That matters for managers, because when one of the three is missing (for example weak rural bandwidth), the whole model can fail.
2 · Definitions 04

IoT is a four-layer stack; the business value sits in the top layer, not the sensor

Internet of Things (IoT): physical objects fitted with sensors and a network connection, so they can report what is happening to them and sometimes be controlled remotely.

Device

A GPS unit on a truck; a fat-content analyser at a milk collection centre.

Connectivity

How data travels. NB-IoT = a low-power mobile network made for small, infrequent readings.

Platform

Where data is stored and cleaned, usually in the cloud.

Application

What a person or machine does differently. This is where money is made.

2 · Definitions

Big Data is data too fast, too large or too messy for a spreadsheet, and only the fifth V pays

Big Data: datasets whose size, speed or variety need special tools to store and analyse. The first three Vs were named by analyst Doug Laney in 2001; Veracity and Value were added later.

VPlain meaningIoT exampleManager's question
VolumeHow muchEvery smart meter reporting every 15 minutesDo we need all of it?
VelocityHow fast it arrives, and how fast we must actA truck's engine alarm needs action in minutesWhat is the decision deadline?
VarietyHow many kinds: numbers, images, text, audioTractor GPS + soil photos + farmer voice notesCan we join them?
VeracityCan we trust it?A dirty or tampered sensor sends wrong readingsWhat if it's wrong?
ValueDoes it change a decision?Service booked before breakdownWhich decision, owned by whom?
Analogy: Big Data is like a monsoon. Volume is how much rain, velocity is how fast it falls, variety is rain, hail and mud together, and veracity is whether the rain gauge works. Value is whether anyone built a reservoir. Without the reservoir, the monsoon is a flood.

3 · The loop

IoT and Big Data are one loop: things create data, data creates decisions, decisions return to the things

IoT → Big Data (steps 1–2)

Thousands of connected assets produce a continuous, high-volume stream of data about how products are actually used. Before this, firms relied on surveys, warranty claims and dealer gossip.

Big Data → IoT (steps 3–4)

Insights flow back to the asset as an alert, a remote shut-off, a changed setting or a price. The value of the data then justifies connecting more assets, and the loop speeds up.

Key insight: a connected product whose data nobody acts on is just a product with a SIM card. The money is made in steps 3–5. Most failed IoT projects stop at step 2.

5 · Value capture — revenue

Every step from selling a product to selling an outcome moves risk from the customer to the maker

Why makers like moving right

Recurring revenue, higher customer lifetime value, and a direct relationship that dealers can't block. Rolls-Royce's "TotalCare" sells jet-engine flying hours, not engines.

Why it's dangerous

If you price per outcome, you absorb breakdowns, misuse and bad weather. You need data good enough to price that risk, and a balance sheet able to carry it.

Servitization: the term (Vandermerwe & Rada, 1988) means bundling services around a product until the service becomes the main source of profit. IoT is what makes the outcome measurable, so it can be priced.
09
5 · Value capture — relationship & advantage

Connection turns an occasional customer into a continuous one, and turns dealers into a strategic problem

Customer relationship

  • Episodic → continuous: contact every day, not at sale and breakdown.
  • Reactive → proactive: the maker calls before the failure.
  • Anonymous → known: the maker sees real usage, not survey answers.
  • Channel conflict: if the maker talks to the farmer directly, what is the dealer for? Many Indian OEMs must design dealer-inclusive models.

Competitive advantage: two views

For: every connected asset improves the prediction models, which attracts more customers, which adds more data. Switching brands means losing your history.

Against: a16z's "The Empty Promise of Data Moats" (Casado & Lauten, 2019) argues that data advantage flattens. After enough data, extra data adds little, and rivals catch up.

Resolution: data is a moat only when it is proprietary, keeps improving the product, and is tied into the customer's workflow.

Back to slide 02: the manufacturer sells kilometres without breakdowns. The truck is now the delivery vehicle for the service, in both senses of the word.

Connected trucks let the maker sell uptime instead of vehicles

Example: Tata Motors' Fleet Edge connected-vehicle platform (similar offers exist from other commercial-vehicle makers).

7 · Indian case B — regulated market

Smart meters let power distributors control losses and invent new tariffs

Context: India's Revamped Distribution Sector Scheme (RDSS, about ₹3 lakh crore) funds prepaid smart meters for distribution companies (discoms).

Before

  • A meter reader visits monthly; many bills are estimated
  • Theft and non-payment are hard to spot
  • The discom learns of an outage from complaints

After

  • Readings arrive automatically; prepaid recharge works like a phone
  • Remote connect/disconnect (the control rung)
  • Time-of-day tariffs and rooftop-solar billing become possible
  • Outages are visible before anyone calls
Competitive advantage in a monopoly? A discom has no rival in its area, so advantage shows up as efficiency and legitimacy: lower commercial losses, better grid planning, and a stronger case in tariff hearings. This contrast with Case A is worth making in an exam answer.
Where it breaks: consumer backlash when prepaid meters disconnect poor households, or when bills jump after accurate metering replaces estimates. Technology without communication creates political risk.

Five ideas to leave with

1 · One loop

IoT creates data; Big Data turns it into action that returns to the asset.

2 · Value is at the top

Sensors become commodities. The money is in decisions and services.

3 · Risk follows revenue

Selling outcomes means owning failures.

4 · Assumptions decide

Connectivity, accuracy, action, acceptance, rights, economics.

5 · Start from a decision

Not "collect everything", which leads to the 1% problem.

Exit question: name one product you own that could be sold to you as a service tomorrow. What would the maker need to know about you, and would you let them?
